What this means

PJM demand was 93,358 MW at the time of this snapshot, 86% of the 108,122 MW peak of the seven days before it. At the latest scored hour (Oct 15, 20:00 UTC), actual demand was 84 MW below the forecast, against a typical miss of about 5,642 MW over the week.

How to read the charts. The blue line is what EIA reports. The green line is what a simple seasonal forecast expected for the same hour: the same hour a week earlier. The residuals chart is the gap between the two, and that gap is the series the detector actually scores.

What a red dot means. A flagged hour is one where that gap looked unusual compared with history, given the time of day and recent behavior. It is not a confirmed outage or event, and there are no ground-truth labels to check it against. 1 hour was flagged in the 7 days before the snapshot, the most recent at Oct 12, 23:00 UTC.

Why a hot afternoon is not an anomaly. The detector scores the gap, not the demand level. A July peak is high but expected, so it is not flagged. Something the forecast did not see coming is.

What it cannot tell you. These are PJM-wide totals, so nothing here can be narrowed to Pennsylvania, a single utility, or a single data center. If data centers are adding load, it would mostly show up as a gradual rise in the baseline, which a forecast that looks back one week absorbs, rather than as individual flagged hours. Weather, electrification and industry move demand too, so no cause is attributed here. For the claims being checked, see the case study; for where reported projects sit, see the PA map.

How fresh it is. EIA publishes hours after the fact and restates figures later, so the newest reading above lags the snapshot time. Recent values can change.[1]
