Are Pennsylvania's utilities delivering on their data center promises?

PPL Electric has told regulators and investors, repeatedly and on the record, how much new power demand is coming. PJM's own grid data is the only honest way to check if that's actually happening.

31.8 GW PPL's own pipeline figure, Q2 2026[1]

Why this exists

This started as a real-time anomaly detector on the PJM grid: an isolation forest scoring forecast residuals across five Pennsylvania utility zones, built and verified end to end (ingestion, Kafka streaming, Kubernetes, CI). That pipeline is live. See the PJM demand page for real output from it, not mockups.

What it's turning into is more specific. PJM's wholesale power market is short on supply, and PJM's own capacity auction is pointing at one cause: AI data center demand.[2][3] Pennsylvania utilities have made public, dated, numbered claims about how much of that demand is coming to their territory. Those claims are checkable against real metered data. This page is the first pass at doing that. The PA map shows where publicly reported data center projects sit relative to each utility.

PPL's pipeline, quarter by quarter

PPL Electric covers roughly 29 counties across central and eastern Pennsylvania, stretching from Lancaster through the Lehigh Valley to Scranton/Wilkes-Barre. It's the one PA utility Pennsylvania's own Public Utility Commission names as facing significant data-center-driven demand growth; the other four zones this project tracks are explicitly not.[4]

Why these numbers might not mean what they say

The duplicate-request problem

A July 2025 London Economics International analysis, prepared for the Southern Environmental Law Center,[8] argues pipeline figures like these are structurally inflated: developers routinely file interconnection requests at multiple candidate sites simultaneously while deciding where to actually build, so the same project can show up in several utilities' "pipeline" totals at once. The same report flags equipment and chip supply bottlenecks as a separate reason real load growth could come in well below what's announced. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's literature review summarizes the same findings.[9]

This is exactly why an accountability check is worth building, not a reason to skip it. There are two competing public stories: explosive, utility-reported growth, and analyst skepticism that it's overstated. Real metered demand is the only neutral referee between them.

Where the real measured comparison lives

The actual PPL load-vs-promises comparison is built and running on real data, but it isn't shown here. PJM's Data Miner 2 API terms prohibit non-member redistribution of data derived from it, and the comparison depends on zone-level LMP and load readings from that feed. It's being tracked internally, not abandoned. Making it public depends on resolving a PJM data redistribution license, a separate piece of work from the pipeline itself.

What this does and doesn't prove

How the detection works: an isolation forest scores the residual between actual demand and a seasonal-naive forecast (same hour, same day of week, prior cycle) plus rolling context, fit only on data through mid-2025 and evaluated on a later window it never trained on. There are no real anomaly labels for this data; detector quality is measured by injecting synthetic anomalies into held-out data and checking recall at a fixed false-positive budget. That's a check on the method, not a guarantee about any specific real event.

Why this is a PPL story, not a five-zone story: Pennsylvania's PUC explicitly says the other four zones tracked here aren't expecting significant data-center impact.[4] Treating this as a statewide comparison would overstate what the data supports.

Correlation, never causation: PA demand can move for reasons that have nothing to do with data centers: weather, broader electrification, EV adoption, ordinary industrial growth. Nothing here is designed or intended to prove a single cause.
