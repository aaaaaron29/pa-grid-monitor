Grid Watch: Working Draft

Are Pennsylvania's utilities delivering on their data center promises?

PPL Electric has told regulators and investors, repeatedly and on the record, how much new power demand is coming. PJM's own grid data is the only honest way to check if that's actually happening.

The case study

Why this exists

This started as a real-time anomaly detector on the PJM grid: an isolation forest scoring forecast residuals across five Pennsylvania utility zones, built and verified end to end (ingestion, Kafka streaming, Kubernetes, CI). That pipeline is live and the numbers below are real output from it, not mockups.

What it's turning into is more specific. PJM's wholesale power market is short on supply, and PJM's own capacity auction is pointing at one cause: AI data center demand. Pennsylvania utilities have made public, dated, numbered claims about how much of that demand is coming to their territory. Those claims are checkable against real metered data. This page is the first pass at doing that.

The other side

Why these numbers might not mean what they say

The duplicate-request problem

A July 2025 London Economics analysis argues pipeline figures like these are structurally inflated: developers routinely file interconnection requests at multiple candidate sites simultaneously while deciding where to actually build, so the same project can show up in several utilities' "pipeline" totals at once. The same report flags equipment and chip supply bottlenecks as a separate reason real load growth could come in well below what's announced.

This is exactly why an accountability check is worth building, not a reason to skip it. There are two competing public stories: explosive, utility-reported growth, and analyst skepticism that it's overstated. Real metered demand is the only neutral referee between them.

The seasonal pattern (winter and summer peaks, spring/fall troughs) is the normal shape of electricity demand and has nothing to do with data centers. What matters is whether that pattern is climbing over time. Through October 2026 it mostly isn't, at least not at zone-wide scale: average and peak load both sit within a few percent of where they were when the pipeline claims started, while the claims themselves grew roughly tenfold.

Why this isn't "PPL is lying"

Signed and pipeline agreements are not the same thing as load already drawing power. Data center interconnection and construction routinely takes several years, and PPL's own numbers distinguish "signed" (11 GW) from "under construction" (6.5 GW) from total pipeline (31.8 GW) for exactly this reason. A zone-wide total also can't separate new data-center load from anything else happening in a 29-county territory. The honest read of this chart isn't "the promises are false," it's "the promised load hasn't landed yet, at the zone-wide level, as of this snapshot." That gap is exactly what this project should keep tracking, quarter over quarter, as more of that 31.8 GW either comes online or doesn't.

One more honesty note on the data itself: PJM's feed never marks PPL's largest load component (PLCO, the bulk of the zone) as settlement-verified, unlike METED, PECO, and PENELEC. Duquesne's zone has the same gap. The numbers above are PJM's real metered readings, just ones PJM itself has not flagged as final.

What's still missing

What would make this airtight

Zone-wide totals can show whether PPL's territory as a whole is growing the way the pipeline claims imply, but they can't show whether any specific signed project has actually been energized. That needs either substation-level data (not public) or a dated, named project list with in-service status, which would have to come from PPL's own interconnection queue or regulatory filings, not PJM's aggregate feed. Until that exists, this page can say "the zone-wide number hasn't moved much yet," not "project X never got built."
Method and honest limits

What this does and doesn't prove

How the detection works: an isolation forest scores the residual between actual demand and a seasonal-naive forecast (same hour, same day of week, prior cycle) plus rolling context, fit only on data through mid-2025 and evaluated on a later window it never trained on. There are no real anomaly labels for this data; detector quality is measured by injecting synthetic anomalies into held-out data and checking recall at a fixed false-positive budget. That's a check on the method, not a guarantee about any specific real event.

Why this is a PPL story, not a five-zone story: Pennsylvania's PUC explicitly says the other four zones tracked here aren't expecting significant data-center impact. Treating this as a statewide comparison would overstate what the data supports.

Why zone-level data can't prove any single project's impact: PPL's territory spans dozens of counties. Even real load-growth data would show "PPL zone-wide," not "this specific facility." Attributing a demand change to one named data center needs substation-level data that isn't public.

Correlation, never causation: PA demand can move for reasons that have nothing to do with data centers: weather, broader electrification, EV adoption, ordinary industrial growth. Nothing here is designed or intended to prove a single cause.
